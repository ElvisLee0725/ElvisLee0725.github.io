# Contact-Method Alternatives for a Static Site

## Summary

The legacy contact form (React form -> Express `/api/formSend` -> SendGrid -> `elvislee0725@gmail.com`) has produced essentially no genuine inquiries in years of operation, per Elvis. That track record is worth taking seriously before defaulting to "just swap SendGrid for another forms-as-a-service provider" — a like-for-like form replacement inherits the same low-signal, spam-exposed surface the old one had, just on a different vendor. This doc compares static-form services (Formspree, Web3Forms, Getform/Forminit, EmailJS) against simpler non-form options (`mailto:`, direct links to LinkedIn/email/Calendly) on cost, setup complexity, account/API-key requirements, spam exposure, and reliability — without picking a winner. That decision belongs to PM/Architect, informed by whether the goal is "maximize chance of a genuine recruiter/client inquiry" (favors low-friction, e.g. direct links) vs. "preserve a form because a form feels more professional/complete" (favors a forms service).

## Option comparison

| Option | Cost at low traffic | Setup complexity | New account/API key tied to Elvis's email? | Spam exposure | Reliability |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **`mailto:` link** (e.g. `mailto:elvislee0725@gmail.com`) | Free | Trivial — one `<a href="mailto:...">` tag, no third party at all | No | None on the site itself; opens the visitor's own mail client, so no server-side attack surface exists. Gmail's own spam filtering still applies to whatever lands in the inbox, same as any public email address. | Depends entirely on the visitor having a configured mail client (a real gap on many mobile browsers / Chromebook-type setups where no default mail app is set) — this is `mailto:`'s one real weakness. |
| **Direct links** (LinkedIn profile, Calendly booking link, plain "email me at ___" text) | Free | Trivial — static anchor links, already partially present (Contact section already links Twitter/Facebook/GitHub/LinkedIn) | No (Calendly free tier optional, not required) | None on-site; whatever spam/DM volume LinkedIn or a Calendly page already attracts, unrelated to this site | Fully reliable — no server, no API, nothing to go down. LinkedIn/Calendly's own uptime applies, both effectively 100% in practice. |
| **Formspree** | Free tier: 50 submissions/month per form, unlimited forms/projects, 30-day submission history, up to 2 notification emails. Paid tiers scale from there. | Low-medium — sign up, get a form endpoint URL, point the existing `<form>`'s `action` at it (or use `fetch`), no server code at all | Yes — new Formspree account, tied to an email for notifications | Formspree includes basic spam filtering (honeypot, reCAPTCHA optional) but it's still a public POST endpoint; some spam gets through on any such service | Third-party dependency; Formspree has been operating for years and is well-established, but adds one more external service in the chain between visitor and Elvis's inbox |
| **Web3Forms** | Free tier: 250 submissions/month, unlimited forms/domains, 30-day history, honeypot + hCaptcha on free tier | Low — sign up for an "access key," embed a hidden input with that key in the form, form posts directly to Web3Forms' endpoint | Yes — requires an access key generated against an email address (Elvis's) | Built-in spam protection (honeypot, hCaptcha) is opt-in but available free, notably more generous than Formspree's free spam tooling | Newer/smaller company than Formspree; free tier is more generous by submission count |
| **Getform (rebranded "Forminit")** | Free tier: 100 submissions/month, 1 form, 50MB storage, spam protection, 30-day archive | Low — similar pattern, form posts to a Getform endpoint URL | Yes — account tied to email | Basic spam protection included on free tier | Underwent a rebrand (Getform -> Forminit) recently; adds a small amount of platform-transition risk/confusion versus more stable competitors |
| **EmailJS** | Free tier: 200 email requests/month, 2 templates, 500 contacts history, 50KB request size cap | Medium — unlike the others, EmailJS sends email directly from the browser using a client-side SDK, which means a **service ID, template ID, and public key all ship in the client bundle** (by design — EmailJS's model relies on this key being public-safe and rate-limited server-side by EmailJS, not secret) | Yes — EmailJS account connected to an actual sending email account (e.g. Gmail via OAuth) | Because the "endpoint" is effectively public in the JS bundle, it's more directly exposed to being found and hammered than a service-generated form action URL; EmailJS mitigates via per-key rate limits but this is a meaningfully different trust model than the others | Established product, but the client-exposed-key model is worth flagging explicitly since it's a different security shape than the other three (server-side form-relay) services |

## Discussion

- **The legacy form's actual failure mode wasn't technical** (it worked, mechanically) — it simply didn't generate genuine leads. Nothing about swapping SendGrid for Formspree/Web3Forms/Getform changes that dynamic; a form is a form from the visitor's perspective. If the goal is *more* genuine contact, a more prominent, lower-friction path (a visible `mailto:` link, a Calendly booking link for anyone who wants to talk, or just a LinkedIn "Message" call-to-action) arguably reduces friction more than moving the same form to a different backend.
- **If a form is kept anyway** (e.g., for aesthetic/professional-completeness reasons, or because Elvis wants submissions logged somewhere before deciding whether to reply), Web3Forms' free tier (250/mo, no per-form cap issue, built-in spam tooling) is the most generous of the three server-relay options surveyed, with Formspree as the more established/longer-track-record alternative at a tighter 50/mo cap. Both require creating a new account under Elvis's email, which is a small but real new maintenance dependency (another login, another vendor whose free tier could change terms).
- **EmailJS's client-exposed-key model is worth a second look before ruling out**, but it is a categorically different risk profile (a permanently public, guessable-if-found endpoint tied to Elvis's real email-sending account) versus the other three, which proxy through a vendor-controlled relay. That distinction should be made explicit if EmailJS is on the table.
- **`mailto:` and direct links have zero ongoing cost, zero new accounts, and zero spam-surface added to the site itself** — the tradeoffs are UX ones (no in-page confirmation state, relies on the visitor's device having a mail client configured for `mailto:` specifically) rather than infrastructure ones. Given the low genuine-inquiry rate historically, this is a legitimate contender to weigh seriously rather than dismiss as "less complete."

## Open questions for PM / Architect

1. Is the goal to preserve an in-page contact form (for perceived completeness/professionalism) or to optimize purely for maximizing genuine inquiries (which may favor direct links)?
2. If a forms service is chosen, is a 50-250 submissions/month free-tier cap acceptable indefinitely, or should cost-at-scale be modeled (unlikely to matter given historical volume, but worth confirming)?
3. If `mailto:`/direct links are chosen instead of a form, should the existing social icon row (Twitter, Facebook, GitHub, LinkedIn) be expanded/reordered to lead with the preferred contact channel?
4. Does the new site want confirmed delivery/logging of who has reached out (something only a forms service or EmailJS can give you), or is that not valued given the historical near-zero genuine response rate?
